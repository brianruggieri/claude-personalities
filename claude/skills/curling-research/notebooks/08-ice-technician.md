# Ice Technician: Pebble, Prep, and Reading

> Scope: the craft of *making* curling ice — flooding, leveling, painting,
> scraping, pebbling, nipping, frost management, mid-game maintenance, and the
> practitioner / certification ecosystem that codifies it. Stone running-band
> friction (Notebook 7), curl-mechanism theory (Notebook 2), and sweeping
> (Notebook 3) are explicitly out of scope and only referenced when the
> *icemaker* must respond to them.

---

## 1. Why curling ice is its own discipline

Curling is the only ice sport played on a deliberately *bumpy* surface. Hockey
ice is shaved smooth; curling ice is sprayed with thousands of frozen droplets
("pebble") that the running band of a 19 kg granite stone rides on. Without
pebble, a curling stone barely curls and barely slides — it sticks. With
pebble, contact area drops by an order of magnitude, meltwater lubricates the
contact, and the stone curves predictably. Every other technique in this
notebook exists to *create* and *preserve* that pebble layer.

> "Curling ice is the hardest one to make among all others, because a very
> very tiny mistake will make a big problem." — Hans Wüthrich, on Beijing 2022
> ([CBC Sports](https://www.cbc.ca/sports/olympics/winter/curling/canadian-icemaker-deals-with-unique-challenges-at-games-1.6336989)).

## 2. Surface build-up (arena conversion)

Brier / Scotties / Olympic events convert a hockey rink to curling in **36–48
hours** under a head ice technician's direction. The sequence is roughly:

1. **Laser-level the slab.** A laser-line transit checks slab flatness; high
   spots are scraped, low spots filled. ([CBC News on Greg Ewasko & the Brier in St. John's](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/newfoundland-labrador/nl-brier-curling-ice-tech-9.7111134))
2. **Build base ice with sequential floods** — typically a multi-thousand-gallon
   bulk flood plus several hose floods to bring the slab close to level
   without trapping air. ([Global News, 2020 Brier Kingston](https://globalnews.ca/news/6595371/2020-brier-preparations-kingston-ahead-of-schedule/))
3. **Paint the ice white** so circles and lines read on TV; then paint house
   rings, hog/back/tee/centre lines, sponsor logos. ([Theater of Sports — Curling Ice Preparation](https://theaterofsports.com/curling/how-curling-ice-is-prepared/))
4. **Seal with a flood**, level again, and grow ice to playing thickness.
5. **Final scrape and pebble** the day before competition; the ice is then
   a tightly tuned object that is barely touched between draws.

Total water and labour budget: a championship sheet is built up from many
thin, fully-frozen layers; rushing layers traps bubbles that show up as soft
spots later in the week.

## 3. The slab climate window

| Variable | Typical championship target | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Ice surface temp | ≈ −3 °C to −5 °C | Cold enough to be hard under the running band, warm enough to release a meltwater film |
| Arena air temp | A few °C above freezing (ice-rink HVAC norm 13–16 °C / 55–60 °F bowl) | Cold air dumps moisture onto the ice |
| Bowl dew point | ≤ ~4 °C / 40 °F (NHL standard 0 °C / 32 °F) | Above the slab dew point, *frost* forms on the ice and stones drag |
| Bowl RH | 30–50 % via desiccant dehumidification | Lower RH = lower frost risk and steadier curl |

Sources: [phcppros — Indoor Ice Rinks: Design Considerations](https://www.phcppros.com/articles/15737-indoor-ice-rinks-design-considerations-for-high-performance-ice);
[Bry-Air — Humidity Solutions for Ice Rinks](https://www.bryair.com/industry/ice-skating-curling-rinks/);
[ASHRAE Refrigeration Handbook — Ice Rinks chapter (2014)](https://www.ashrae.org/technical-resources/ashrae-handbook).
The icemaker rides these dials all day; a 1 °C dew-point creep is enough to
slow a championship sheet visibly.

> Mark Callan calls frost "a killer for curling stones … it can slow the
> stones and affect their path so they don't curl evenly."
> ([NBC Philadelphia — Cortina ice tech Mark Callan](https://www.nbcphiladelphia.com/olympics/2026-milan-cortina/mark-callan-michael-jackson-of-curling-cortina-ice-tech/4350327/))

## 4. Pebbling — the craft inside the craft

Pebbling is sprinkling near-freezing water in a controlled pattern so droplets
freeze on contact into rounded bumps a few millimetres across.

### 4.1 Equipment

- **Pebble can** with a brass head; the head is a perforated disc whose hole
  count and hole geometry set the droplet size. Hans Wüthrich's team famously
  travels with **25 different pebble heads** for tuning.
  ([Hans Wuthrich — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Wuthrich_(icemaker)))
- **Numbered heads** by convention: a "fat" head (e.g. 74) makes fewer, larger
  drops; a "fine" head (e.g. 76) makes many, smaller drops. Two-pass
  conventions usually combine a coarser first pass with a finer top pass.
  ([Curling Baron — Pebble/Nip](https://curlingbaron.wordpress.com/making-ice/);
  [Little Kiwi Gone Curling — The Art of Pebbling](https://littlekiwigonecurling.wordpress.com/2020/01/24/the-ice-apprentice-the-art-of-pebbling/))

### 4.2 Water spec

- **Hot water, ≈ 35 °C** at the can, hitting cold ice. Hot water releases
  dissolved gas, freezes more uniformly, and gives a rounder pebble than cold
  tap water. ([Theater of Sports](https://theaterofsports.com/curling/how-curling-ice-is-prepared/))
- **Purity matters**: many championship icemakers use deionised or
  demineralised water so dissolved minerals don't seed irregular crystals.
- Shorty Jenkins explicitly tested *tap, rain, filtered, hot, and cold*
  water — the modern hot-deionised default came out of that work.
  ([Wikipedia — Shorty Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorty_Jenkins))

### 4.3 Drop size and shape

- Target droplet size at impact: **≈ 2–3 mm**, freezing into a roughly
  hemispherical bump. ([Curling Basics — Ice](https://www.curlingbasics.com/en/the-ice.html))
- Two coats are typical: a *cooler* base coat that bonds to the slab, then a
  slightly warmer top coat that "stands taller" and is what the first stone
  of the game touches. If the top coat wears through during a long game, the
  base coat is the safety net. ([Smithsonian Magazine — Why Curling Ice Is
  Different](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/why-curling-ice-is-different-than-other-ice-180949752/))

### 4.4 Walking pattern

The icemaker walks the sheet swinging the can in a metronomic arc — Mark
Callan's signature backward-walking, side-to-side motion got him the
"Michael Jackson of curling" / "moonwalk" nickname at Milano-Cortina 2026.
([Olympics.com — Mark Callan moonwalk](https://www.olympics.com/en/milano-cortina-2026/news/winter-olympics-curling-technician-pebbling-science-mark-callan-moonwalk))
The walk is timed so droplets fall *evenly* on every square cm of sheet —
uneven distribution is the single biggest source of inconsistent curl.

### 4.5 Pebble life across a game

- **Ends 1–2:** new pebble — surface is *slow* and *swingy*. Stones grip
  bumps hard; curl is largest; speed is lowest.
- **Ends 3–6:** pebble flattens into "plateaus" (sweeping accelerates this).
  Speed increases. Curl narrows. This is the most-predictable window — the
  reason ice technicians talk about ice "settling in."
  ([Chaska Curling Center — Cleaning and Nipping the Ice](https://chaskacurlingcenter.com/cleaning-nipping-ice/))
- **Ends 7+:** if no maintenance break, pebble is worn / contaminated; speed
  ticks back down (frost-y feel), curl flattens, lines drift. Championship
  events therefore re-pebble between games, never during.
  ([Curling Wikipedia article — Ice section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curling#Ice))

## 5. Scraping and nipping

Scraping is the icemaker's "delete" key.

- Between games at championship level, ice crews **8-pass scrape** the entire
  surface with a flat-blade scraper, then re-pebble. The 8-pass gets the
  surface back to fully bare ice.
  ([Chaska Curling Center](https://chaskacurlingcenter.com/cleaning-nipping-ice/);
  [Curling Baron — Scraping](https://curlingbaron.wordpress.com/tips-tricks-2/))
- **Nipping** is a *very* shallow scrape — it slices the tops off freshly
  laid pebble, turning hemispheres into plateaus of equal height. Nipping
  is the *final* step of pre-game prep: it stabilises pebble height and
  is what sets opening-end speed and curl.
- Target nip yield is roughly **45–50 g of ice shavings per sheet** — that is
  the order of "you barely took anything." A dull blade smashes pebble
  instead of cutting it and produces a slow start, fast middle, dead end.
  ([Little Kiwi Gone Curling — Tricky Nipping](https://littlekiwigonecurling.wordpress.com/2020/03/10/the-ice-apprentice-tricky-nipping/))
- Nipping is back-line to back-line only; the slide path, not the house, is
  what curlers care about most.
- Wüthrich is the recognised authority on scraper-blade design and
  sharpening — his 2003 Canadian Curling Association *Award of Achievement*
  was specifically for "development of new pebble heads and ice scraper
  technology." ([Hans Wuthrich — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Wuthrich_(icemaker));
  [Eye on the Ice](https://www.eyeontheice.com/iceconsultinginternational.htm) is his
  consulting / blade-sharpening business.)

## 6. Reading the ice — how technicians measure what they made

### 6.1 Speed: hog-to-hog timing

The dominant ice-speed metric is the **hog-to-hog (HTH) stopwatch reading**:
seconds between the stone crossing the near hog line and the far hog line on
a draw that comes to rest near the tee. Higher number = faster ice.

| Ice character | HTH (s) |
|---|---|
| Slow club ice | 12–13 |
| Normal | 13.5–14.2 |
| Fast / championship | 14–16 |

([CurlTech — Timing Rocks](https://www.curltech.com/curling-training/timing-rocks);
[Lanark Curling Club timing tech sheet](https://www.lanarkcurlingclub.org/wp-content/uploads/Support_Tech5-Timing.pdf);
[Rookie Road — Curling Split Times](https://www.rookieroad.com/curling-split-times-3879433/))

A separate tradition cited by Olympics.com / NBC and many club programs uses
**total time-down-ice** (release to stop) measured in "seconds of ice" — a
*24-second* sheet is fast (the rock takes 24 s draw weight), a *19-second*
sheet is slow. Olympic / Brier ice is generally "24.5–25 second ice."
Technicians use both metrics: HTH is portable across players and arenas;
seconds-of-ice is good for in-room signage.

### 6.2 Curl: feet-of-curl

Reported as the lateral offset (in **feet**) between a draw's release line
and where it stops. Championship targets are commonly **4–6 feet of curl**
on a clean draw to the button — bigger curl means more strategic options.
Curl drifts during a game as pebble flattens; the icemaker's job is to
keep that drift inside a window narrow enough that skips can trust their
calls. ([Wikipedia — Curling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curling);
[CurlTech — Ice and Rocks](https://www.curltech.com/curling-training/ice-and-rocks))

### 6.3 Path memory and ice "negotiation"

As stones repeatedly run lines, pebble in those tracks wears faster than
unused ice between them. Skips and ice techs both *read* this:
heavily-used paths get faster but lose curl (less unbroken pebble to grab);
unused outside paths stay swingy. A skip choosing to "rub a guard wide" is
also choosing to put a stone on a fresh track that hasn't been worn down.
The icemaker counters by varying nip patterns and draw-shot distribution
during practice so wear is symmetric. (Cross-referenced from
[Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/why-curling-ice-is-different-than-other-ice-180949752/)
and [Chaska Curling Center](https://chaskacurlingcenter.com/cleaning-nipping-ice/);
the formal term "path memory" is informal.)

## 7. Frost — the operating-room hazard

Frost forms when the slab is *colder* than the bowl dew point, so atmospheric
moisture sublimates onto the ice. Frosted ice feels slow and unpredictable —
stones plough little dust ridges instead of riding pebble. The icemaker's
frost playbook:

1. **Dew-point first.** Run the bowl drier (more desiccant air) before
   running the slab colder.
2. **Air movement.** Stagnant cold pockets above the slab condense moisture;
   slow circulation fans help.
3. **Towel / dry-mop the surface** between draws if frost has set in
   ("how the ice crew has taken care of frosty curling conditions at the
   Brier" — [Yahoo News on Brier 2022](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/ice-crew-taken-care-frosty-010152761.html)).
4. **Don't open big doors.** A loading bay opening behind the rink during a
   draw can dump enough humid air to wreck the next end.

## 8. Mid-game maintenance breaks

In a 10-end championship game (or 8-end round-robin) crews typically take a
**5-minute break after the 5th end**. During that window the icemaker can:

- Run a warm towel over a frosty patch.
- Take a stopwatch reading and confirm HTH hasn't drifted.
- *Not* re-pebble — pebble during a game is unheard of at championship level
  (it changes the playing surface mid-match). Re-pebble only happens between
  games.

Source: Curling Canada operations notes, cross-referenced with
[Mike Bryson, "Maintaining Curling Ice" (RFABC, 2007)](https://rfabc.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/curlmain.pdf)
which documents typical mid-game routines for club programs.

## 10. Olympic-grade vs club-grade ice — what actually differs

| Dimension | Olympic / Brier | Healthy club |
|---|---|---|
| Slab flatness | Laser-leveled to ≤1–2 mm tolerance across 45 m | Typically 5–10 mm variation |
| Speed (HTH) | 14.5–16 s (24–25 s ice) | 12.5–14 s (19–22 s ice) |
| Curl | 4–6 ft of curl, predictable across game | 2–4 ft, often asymmetric L vs R |
| Pebble passes | Two coats, 25 candidate heads, deionised water | One coat, 1–2 heads, tap or filtered water |
| Maintenance | 8-pass scrape + nip + re-pebble *between every game* | Scrape between draws, nip every few sessions |
| Climate | Bowl dehumidified to <40 % RH; slab held to ±0.2 °C | Often ambient, slab drifts ±1 °C with weather |
| Operator | World Curling Academy Chief Ice Technician + crew of 4–8 | One Level-1/2 volunteer per night |

The Beijing 2022 *Ice Cube* venue was an extreme outlier — converted from a
swimming pool, the venue's humidity was so low at altitude that Wüthrich's
team actually had to *humidify* the bowl rather than dehumidify it.
([CBC Sports](https://www.cbc.ca/sports/olympics/winter/curling/canadian-icemaker-deals-with-unique-challenges-at-games-1.6336989);
[Xinhua coverage](https://english.news.cn/20220129/cafafe6902c940159214f1ee5449716a/c.html))

## 11. Implications for a curling simulator

A simulator that wants to reflect ice-tech reality (not just stone physics)
should expose at minimum:

- **Per-game ice age:** speed (HTH) and curl evolve across ends. Open-end
  curl > mid-game curl > worn-end curl, with a small frost-back-up at the
  end if no maintenance break.
- **Pebble field, not a uniform plane.** Stochastic per-cell pebble height
  with a global mean that drifts with use. Used paths wear faster (this
  notebook's "path memory").
- **Climate dial** the player can't see directly but that drives speed/curl
  drift: dew-point margin, slab temp delta, frost rate.
- **Maintenance breaks** as a discrete game-state event that resets a
  fraction of pebble wear.
- **Championship-vs-club preset** (Section 10 table) — the same physics with
  different parameter envelopes.

The right granularity is *the icemaker's mental model*, not a CFD of the slab.
HTH-in-seconds, feet-of-curl, and dew-point-margin are the three numbers a
real championship technician watches. A sim that surfaces those three is
already deeper than 95 % of curling games on the market.
