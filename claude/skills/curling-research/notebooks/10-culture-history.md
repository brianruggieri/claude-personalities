## 1. Origins (16th–18th c. Scotland)

- **World Curling — official history.** Federation-published timeline: 1540 Paisley Abbey challenge between monk John Sclater and Gavin Hamilton (recorded by notary John McQuhin); Bruegel paintings depicting an icehouse-style game; lineage from Scottish lochs to the modern indoor sheet. Canonical for any "since [year]" claim.
  - https://worldcurling.org/about/history/
- **The Stirling stone (1511).** A curling stone inscribed `1511` was recovered when an old pond was drained at Dunblane; held at the Stirling Smith Art Gallery & Museum. Oldest physical evidence of the sport. The Smith's curating notes are the primary source — search "Stirling stone" on the museum site.
  - https://www.smithartgalleryandmuseum.co.uk/
- **Kilsyth Curling Club (formally constituted 1716).** Claims status as the world's first curling club; still operating. Useful for "world's oldest" lore lines and any "since 1716" callouts.
- **Etymology of "curling" — first print 1620.** Henry Adamson's poem "The Muses Threnodie" (Perth, 1620) contains the earliest printed use of the verb. For deeper word histories use the **Dictionary of the Scots Language** (https://dsl.ac.uk/) — it has entries for `curl`, `bonspiel` (probably Dutch `bond` + `spel`, "league play"), `hog`, `hack`, and `tee`.
- **Smith, David B. — *Curling: An Illustrated History*** (Edinburgh: John Donald, 1981). The standard scholarly history of the sport's first 400 years. Cited by World Curling, Royal Caledonian, and the National Museum of Scotland. Authoritative for any pre-1900 detail. Held by the National Library of Scotland; not freely online — query NotebookLM if you need a specific fact.

## 2. Codification & the Royal Caledonian (1838–1843)

- **Grand Caledonian Curling Club founded 1838 in Edinburgh** "for the purpose of regulating the ancient Scottish game of Curling by general laws." Received royal patronage by 1842/43 after Queen Victoria watched a demonstration on the ballroom floor at Scone Palace, becoming the **Royal Caledonian Curling Club** ("the Mother Club"). First codified rule set is the ancestor of every modern federation rulebook.
  - World Curling history: https://worldcurling.org/about/history/
  - Scottish Curling: https://www.scottishcurling.org/about/history/
- **First indoor Scottish rink: Crossmyloof, Glasgow (1907).** Marks the transition from outdoor pond curling to year-round indoor play. Useful texture for "the way the game used to be played" voice-overs.

## 3. Expansion to Canada (1807 onward)

- **Royal Montreal Curling Club founded 27 January 1807** at the Gillis Tavern by Scottish-Canadian immigrants. Oldest active sports club of any kind in North America. Built Canada's first indoor ice rink ~1837.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Montreal_Curling_Club
- **Scottish immigration as the transmission vector.** 19th-century Scottish settlers carried iron-handled "irons" and granite stones into Quebec, Ontario, and Atlantic Canada, then west with the railway. Curling Canada's history page (`curling.ca/about-the-sport/history/`) is the federation primary; if blocked behind 403, query NotebookLM with "Curling Canada history origins immigration."
- **Bob Weeks — *Curling for Dummies*** (Wiley, multiple eds.). Historical chapters cover the Canadian club lineage, the irons-to-granite transition, and the cultural absorption of curling into Prairie identity. Weeks is also the Globe & Mail / The Curling News columnist of record — his long-form essays are reliable Canadian-context sources.
- **Maxwell, Doug — *Tales of a Curling Hack*** (Whitecap, 2003). First-person social history of mid-century Canadian curling: club hospitality, the "broom-stacking" tradition, the Brier circuit, broadcast-era personalities. Good for warm club-life flavor and for the etymology of broomstacking (originally: stacking your corn brooms in the corner before drinks; now: any post-game social ritual).

## 4. The Brier (men's national, est. 1927)

- **Founded 1927 by Macdonald Tobacco** at the Granite Club in Toronto. The name "Brier" comes from Macdonald's Brier tobacco brand — a brand-of-record naming convention that survived all subsequent sponsor changes (Labatt 1980–2000, Nokia 2001–04, Tim Hortons 2005–23, Montana's 2024–).
- **Format evolution.** 14 ends (1927) → 12 ends (1928) → 10 ends (1977). Today an 18-team field with provincial/territorial representation; winner represents Canada at the men's Worlds. Crowd attendance routinely exceeds Worlds-in-Canada attendance.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_(curling)
- **Tankard / Purple Heart traditions.** Brier winners receive the Macdonald Brier Tankard (now Tim Hortons Brier Tankard) and a "Purple Heart" crest — a Canadian curling honour with 90+ years of accumulated weight. Worth name-checking in trophy-screen copy.

## 5. The Scotties / Women's National (1961 onward)

- **Canadian Ladies' Curling Association Championship 1961.** Direct descendant of the 1960 Diamond D invitational won by Joyce McKee. Ran under several sponsor names: **Macdonald Lassie** (1972–79), **Scott Tournament of Hearts** (1982–2006), and the modern **Scotties Tournament of Hearts** (2007–) after Kruger acquired Scott Paper licensing. The "Hearts" branding came from Robin Wilson's pitch to Scott Paper.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotties_Tournament_of_Hearts

## 7. Famous Skips, Teams, and Shots

Use these as ready-made lore beats — each is well-documented and not invented:

- **Sandra Schmirler (1963–2000)** — skipped Canada to the inaugural women's Olympic gold (Nagano 1998). Three-time World Champion (1993/94/97). Front page of *The New York Times*. Died of cancer at 36; the **Sandra Schmirler Foundation** (est. 2001) funds NICUs across Canada. Canonical "legacy" reference for the women's game.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandra_Schmirler
- **Brad Gushue** — Olympic gold 2006 Turin (then 25, youngest gold-medal skip); record Brier titles thereafter.
- **Russ Howard** — Innovator of the "free guard zone" rule (the "Howard Rule," 1990s) and Gushue's Olympic third in 2006 at age 50; voice of curling intuition.
- **Kevin Martin ("the Old Bear")** — 2010 Olympic gold (Vancouver), four-time Brier; technical icon of the men's game.
- **Jennifer Jones** — Six-time Canadian champion; **first undefeated Olympic curling gold (Sochi 2014, 11–0)**; later the only woman with both an Olympic gold and a perfect Olympic record.
- **Anette Norberg (SWE)** — Back-to-back Olympic gold 2006/2010; first repeat women's gold.
- **Eve Muirhead (GBR/SCO)** — 2022 Beijing women's gold; modern face of British curling.
- **Niklas Edin (SWE)** — Most decorated men's skip of the 2010s–2020s; 2022 Beijing gold; multi-time World Champion.

For shot-by-shot detail, the **CBC Sports** and **TSN** archives plus the **World Curling YouTube channel** carry full footage. Reference videos rather than inventing shot descriptions.

## 8. National Rivalries & Identity

- **Canada** — the cultural homeland. Curling is woven into Prairie identity (especially Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Alberta). Brier as civic event.
- **Sweden / Norway / Switzerland / Scotland** — the European core. Sweden produces technical champions (Norberg, Edin). Norway is famous on the international stage for the **Norwegian men's pants** — Loudmouth-brand harlequin trousers worn since 2010 Vancouver, now an iconic Olympic-curling visual meme.
- **South Korea / Japan** — Asian powerhouses since the 2010s. "Garlic Girls" (Team Kim, Gyeonggi Province) became a 2018 PyeongChang cultural phenomenon for Korea.
- **USA** — long underdog tradition broken by **John Shuster's "Rejects" team** at PyeongChang 2018 (10–7 over Sweden in the final), the first US Olympic curling gold.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shuster

## 9. Terminology Etymology

For lore lines, hint copy, and tooltip flavor:

- **Skip** — from Scots, abbreviation of "skipper" (ship's captain). The skip "captains" the rink.
- **Hack** — Scots for a notch or foothold cut into ice; once literally chopped, now a rubber-and-steel block.
- **Hammer** — the final stone of an end. Hammer = "decisive blow." Modern usage; not in the 1838 RCCC rules.
- **Hog / hog line** — from Scots farming: a "hog" was a weak lamb culled before its first winter. A stone that fails to cross the far hog line is "hogged" — i.e., culled.
- **Bonspiel** — Scots, probably from Dutch `bond` (league) + `spel` (game / play). League-play tournament. Recorded use from the 16th century.
- **Button** — the centre circle (1-foot / ~30 cm radius). Originally the literal small disc marking dead centre.
- **House** — the four-ring target. Term predates 1838.
- **Sheet** — the playing surface itself. Distinguishes the lane from the broader rink (the building).
- **Rink** — confusingly, both the building and the four-player team. The team usage is older (Scots: a course/lane, then by extension the players using it).
- **End** — the period of play (one direction down the sheet). 8 or 10 ends in modern competition.
- **Pebble / pebbling** — the bumpy ice texture sprayed warm-water droplets that freeze into a roughened surface, enabling curl. Pre-game pebbling is itself a ritual (see §10).

Primary sources: **OED**, **Dictionary of the Scots Language** (https://dsl.ac.uk/), and Smith, *Curling: An Illustrated History*, ch. 1–3.

## 10. Etiquette, Rituals, and the Spirit of Curling

This is the lore that distinguishes curling from every other major sport. Lean into it for narration and onboarding.

- **The Spirit of Curling** — a written code in the World Curling rulebook. Key tenets: never cheer an opponent's miss; modest acknowledgement of your own good shots; concede graciously when the game is decided; offer "Good curling" handshakes before AND after every game; the winner traditionally **buys the loser's first drink** post-game.
- **Pre-game ritual: pebbling.** The ice technician walks the sheet with a pebbling can, fanning fine warm-water droplets that freeze into the textured surface. The sound of pebbling and the smell of warm pond-ice are the sensory signature of arriving at a curling club. (See Notebook 8 for the technical side.)
- **Pipe-band openings.** Major Scottish, Canadian, and World championships open with a piper leading teams onto the sheet. Standard at the Brier, Scotties, and Worlds.
- **Broomstacking.** Originally: after a game, sweepers stacked their corn brooms in the corner of the club lounge before drinks. Now: any post-game social ritual where teams share a drink (winner buys). The term survives in Maxwell's *Tales of a Curling Hack*.
- **Concession.** A skip ends a game by extending a hand and saying "good curling" or "we'll shake." There is no clock-running-out; the loser literally walks off. Treated as honourable, not surrender.
- **Club hospitality.** Most curling clubs operate as social clubs: the lounge ("the warm room") is as central as the sheet. Spiel weekends are tournaments + parties + meals together for 48 hours.
- **Norway's pants.** Since Vancouver 2010, the Norwegian men's team has worn Loudmouth Golf harlequin/diamond/argyle trousers — an Olympic visual signature, an early sports-meme, and a deliberate "we are having fun" cultural statement.
- **The Garlic Girls (KOR, 2018).** Team Kim from Gyeonggi nicknamed themselves after their hometown's garlic-farming region; "Yogurt!" (Skip Kim Eun-jung's call to Kim Yeong-mi) became a viral catchphrase. Useful texture for any non-Western voice in the sim.

## 12. Museums & Heritage Sites

- **Stirling Smith Art Gallery & Museum** (Stirling, Scotland) — holds the Stirling Stone (1511) and curling artefacts.
- **National Museum of Scotland** (Edinburgh) — curling stones from Ailsa Craig, irons, brushes; covers Scottish sporting heritage.
- **Canadian Curling Hall of Fame** (Curling Canada) — induction lists, archived photos. Online roster: https://www.curling.ca/about-the-sport/awards-honours/canadian-curling-hall-of-fame/
- **World Curling Hall of Fame** — federation-curated; includes Schmirler, Howard, Martin, Jones, Norberg, Edin, etc.
- **Royal Caledonian Curling Club archives** (Edinburgh) — primary documents from 1838 onwards; queryable through the RCCC office. Site has had recurring TLS issues; reach via https://www.scottishcurling.org/.
- **Royal Montreal Curling Club** — the building itself (1850 De Maisonneuve W., Montreal) is a heritage site; oldest active sports club in North America.

## Source Quality Tiering

**Primary (federation/museum/scholarly):** World Curling history, RCCC archives, Curling Canada, Scottish Curling, Stirling Smith Museum, National Museum of Scotland, Smith *Illustrated History*.

**Strong secondary (long-form journalism/standard reference):** Bob Weeks (Globe & Mail, The Curling News, *Curling for Dummies* historical chapters), Doug Maxwell *Tales of a Curling Hack*, NFB documentaries, World Curling YouTube retrospectives, CBC archives.

**Acceptable (named etymology / one canonical link):** OED, Dictionary of the Scots Language, the Wikipedia entries linked above (used as index pointers — verify any specific date or quote against a primary before it ships in user-visible copy).

**Banned in this notebook:** SEO listicles, AI-generated history blurbs, uncited "fun fact" sites. If a claim cannot be traced to a primary or strong secondary, drop it or mark it explicitly as folklore.
